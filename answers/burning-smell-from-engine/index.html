Burning Smell From Your Engine

The short answer

The smell tells you a lot. Sweet and syrupy means coolant, which points at a leak onto something hot. Acrid and sharp like burnt plastic suggests electrical. Hot oil smells heavy and lingers. Burning rubber usually means a slipping belt. Any burning smell paired with smoke means stop and switch off.

Is it safe to drive?

It depends entirely on which smell. Electrical burning or any visible smoke means stop now. A faint hot-oil smell after a long drive is worth investigating but not an emergency. If in doubt, pull over — the cost of being wrong is high.

Sweet and syrupy: coolant

Coolant has an unmistakably sweet smell. If you can smell it, it is escaping and landing somewhere hot enough to vaporise it — often a hose, the radiator, or a heater core. If you smell it inside the cabin with a greasy film on the inside of the windscreen, the heater core is leaking.

Sharp and acrid: electrical

Burning plastic or an ozone-like sharpness suggests wiring insulation overheating. This is the one to take most seriously, because the failure mode is fire. Stop, switch off, and do not keep driving to see whether it goes away.

Heavy and oily: oil on the exhaust

An oil leak dripping onto hot exhaust produces a distinctive heavy smell and sometimes light smoke from under the bonnet. Valve cover gaskets are a common culprit. Not usually an emergency, but oil on a hot manifold is a genuine fire risk over time.

Burning rubber

Usually a slipping accessory belt, or a hose that has come to rest against something hot. Occasionally a seized brake caliper dragging, in which case you may also notice one wheel much hotter than the others after a drive.

Related questions

New car smells hot for the first few drives — normal?

Often yes. Protective coatings on new exhaust components burn off and produce a smell for a short period.

Burning smell after driving through deep water?

Usually debris caught against the exhaust. Worth a look underneath before assuming something failed.
